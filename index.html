01 — About

Engineering as craft, not output

I started in 2017 writing gameplay code for small Android titles, where the feedback loop was brutally honest: if an interaction felt wrong, the store reviews said so within a day. That taught me to treat user experience as an engineering problem rather than a coat of paint.

Since then the work has moved deeper into systems. At nCore Games I owned the decision-making AI, statistics, and mathematics layers of an officially licensed cricket simulation released globally — probabilistic behaviour translated into deterministic, testable code. At Vasundhara Vision I led a small team and built the multiplayer backend behind real-money sessions with Node.js, Express and Socket.IO, where a dropped connection is not a bug report, it is a financial dispute.

Today, at ViitorCloud Technologies, I design modular C# systems for real-time, multi-user platforms — authoritative networking, state synchronisation, REST and third-party SDK integration, and the profiling work that keeps large 3D scenes inside a mobile memory budget. Reusable architecture cut our feature delivery time by roughly 30%, which is the kind of number I like: it comes from restraint, not heroics.

I use AI tooling every day — code generation, refactoring, test scaffolding, documentation, and increasingly MCP-connected workflows. It is a force multiplier on the parts of the job that were always mechanical. The judgement about what to build, how to shape a boundary, and which trade-off to accept is still mine, and that is the part I keep sharpening.

How I work

  • Boundaries first. Small modules with honest interfaces. OOP and SOLID as tools, not ceremony.
  • Make it observable. Structured error handling, retries, and logs that explain themselves at 2 a.m.
  • Determinism where it counts. Behaviour you can unit test and tune beats behaviour you can only admire.
  • Review as mentoring. CI-gated pull requests, shared standards, fewer defects reaching QA.
  • Keep learning in public. New tooling adopted deliberately, measured against real delivery outcomes.
